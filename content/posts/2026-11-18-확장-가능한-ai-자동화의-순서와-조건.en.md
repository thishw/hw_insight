The fact that something can be automated does not, by itself, prove the value of automating it. To achieve both scalability and tangible results, organizations should first standardize and optimize high-volume, stable, and measurable work, then begin by automating repetitive tasks.

Customer touchpoints and context-dependent judgment should remain in human hands. The success of automation should be judged not by the number of implementations, but by net efficiency—cost savings and quality improvements minus total input costs—and by how quickly an organization learns to scale proven results.

> Scalable AI automation should proceed in the order of standardization, optimization, and automation.  
> It should begin with work that is repetitive, measurable, high-volume, and stable.  
> Only automation with demonstrated net efficiency should be scaled, while preserving human judgment.

## Economic Value Comes Before Automation Potential

The pace of automation in knowledge work, including coding, is likely to accelerate further. But saying that something can be automated technically is entirely different from saying that it is worth automating from a business perspective.

Automation valuation is the process of determining economic value by comparing outcomes generated by automation—such as cost savings, faster processing, and improved quality—with the total cost of implementation. This value cannot be determined merely by counting automation initiatives or whether automation has been adopted.

Can an organization’s net efficiency decline even as its automation rate rises? Absolutely. If development, computing, maintenance, and validation costs exceed the savings, the number of automation tools may grow while business performance deteriorates.

The principles for AI-first organizations summarized by news.hada.io also explain that rewards should be tied to automation that delivers real results, rather than to automation itself or vanity metrics. Personally, I believe organizations that can quickly discontinue ineffective automation are likely to be stronger than those that simply adopt more automation.

## From Standardization to Optimization, and From Optimization to Automation

Not Boring introduced Senra Systems’ approach, which calls for proceeding in the order of “standardize, optimize, automate.” This sequence is not just a slogan; it is the foundation for scaling AI automation across multiple teams and products.

Standardization means ensuring that the same work can be performed and evaluated using similar procedures and criteria, regardless of who carries it out. Not only work processes but also communication methods and organizational culture must be aligned to some degree.

Standardized processes reveal waste such as bottlenecks, redundant approvals, and unnecessary movement. Optimizing procedures and costs at this stage helps prevent the mistake of automating inefficient work as-is.

A school’s grading process offers an easy analogy. If every teacher uses different answers and scoring criteria, introducing an automated grading system will only accelerate confusion. But if the grading criteria are standardized first, the same system can be replicated across multiple classes.

Ultimately, the higher the level of standardization, the easier it is to apply automated work to other teams and products. Scaling begins not only with the performance of automation tools, but with repeatable processes.

## Criteria for Choosing What to Automate First

The first target for automation should be work for which success and failure can be clearly determined. Not every problem can be automated at once; automation is more likely to deliver rapid benefits when applied first to problems defined in measurable terms.

The following criteria are the minimum requirements for screening automation candidates.

| Criterion | Conditions Suitable for Scaling | Question to Ask |
|---|---|---|
| Measurability | Success and failure must be judged using consistent criteria. | Can the correct result or an acceptable range be defined? |
| Repetitiveness | The same type of task must occur continuously. | Can the procedure repeatedly performed by people be expressed as rules? |
| Scale | The workload must be large enough to recoup the investment. | Are the throughput and potential time savings sufficient? |
| Stability | Inputs and procedures must not change too frequently. | Is repeated redesign during operations unlikely? |
| Economics | The expected value of efficiency gains must exceed the total input cost. | Does the initiative remain profitable after development, manufacturing, software, and operating costs are deducted? |

Repetitive inspections with clear criteria, such as reviews, are good candidates for early automation. If tools and infrastructure are configured so that multiple AI agents divide the inspection criteria among themselves and validate one another’s results, people can be freed from the drudgery of repetitive reviews.

However, not everything called a review can be automated. AI can handle checks for compliance with rules, but humans should interpret ambiguous context and make judgments for which they are accountable.

## Resolving the Tension Between Rapid Experimentation and Upfront Standardization

If standardization and optimization must come first, does that mean giving up rapid launches and learning? No. The scope of these two principles can be determined according to the cost of reversing an automation failure.

| Work Conditions | Appropriate Approach | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Work with high volatility and high failure costs | Automate only after sufficient standardization and optimization. | The impact of incorrect execution on customers and operations must be reduced. |
| Work that is easy to test on a small scale and reverse | Launch quickly within a limited scope. | Real-world data can be collected early to validate assumptions. |
| Work that has demonstrated initial results | Incorporate experimental findings into standard procedures before scaling. | Lessons learned must be converted into repeatable operating practices. |

The AI-first organizational approach summarized by news.hada.io emphasizes designing work to reach the launch and learning stages as quickly as possible. By rapidly launching small automation initiatives and feeding the resulting error and cost data back into standard procedures, standardization and learning can be connected in a continuous cycle.

Of course, this judgment may be wrong. That is why it seems safer to validate assumptions through experiments with a limited blast radius rather than scaling company-wide from the outset.

## The Boundaries of Automation and the Human Role

An AI-first organization is not simply one that replaces people with AI. It is an organization that reduces unnecessary organizational boundaries and incorporates AI throughout the entire workflow while positioning people at customer touchpoints and wherever accountable judgment is required.

News.hada.io explains that although AI-first organizations should automate proactively, they should also hire people where necessary and ensure that humans handle customer interactions and judgment. This means expanding the scope of automation is not the same as eliminating the human role.

AI is well suited to handling burdens such as repetitive execution, rule-based inspections, and result comparison. Humans should focus on decisions that require interpreting exceptions, communicating with customers, and taking responsibility for outcomes.

In the same vein, the drudgery of reviews should be automated, but responsibility for them should not be handed over to machines. Checks with clear evaluation criteria should be automated, while decisions that change the criteria themselves or approve exceptions should remain with humans.

## Drive Continuous Improvement Through Net Efficiency and Learning Speed

Automation performance should be managed based on net efficiency, not gross savings. Net efficiency is the value of cost savings and productivity and quality improvements minus computing, development, maintenance, operating, and validation costs.

Computing costs must be continuously optimized. Meanwhile, Meta has proposed that processes must be continually updated to keep pace with innovation and that sufficient computing resources should be allocated to areas of self-improvement necessary to remain competitive.

These two arguments may seem contradictory, but they apply under different conditions. Costs should be reduced in areas where the economics of repetitive work are paramount, while learning outcomes may take precedence over short-term costs in areas where self-improvement is critical to competitiveness.

Ultimately, automation investment should repeat the following cycle in rapid succession.

**Launch small.**

Automate measurable work within a limited scope.

**Measure real-world results.**

Determine net efficiency by subtracting all input costs from savings and quality improvements.

**Update procedures.**

Incorporate errors and exceptions discovered during experiments into standardized processes.

**Scale only proven automation.**

Replicate work with demonstrated results across other teams and products.

Scalable AI automation should begin by standardizing high-volume, stable, and measurable work, optimizing costs and processes, and then applying automation first to repetitive tasks. Customer touchpoints and context-dependent judgment should remain with humans, while small experiments accelerate learning and continuously verify whether net efficiency exceeds input costs.

The fact that something can be automated is only a starting condition. The ultimate value of automation appears to depend on how quickly an organization learns from actual results and how safely it replicates them.

## One-Line Comment.

AI automation is not about buying a faster car. It is about improving the roads, deciding who will be behind the wheel, and then accelerating first on the stretches that generate a return.
